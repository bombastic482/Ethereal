Every diet that has ever worked, from keto to intermittent fasting to plain portion control, worked for the same reason: it got you to eat fewer calories than your body used. That is the calorie deficit, and it is the one non-negotiable of fat loss. The problem is that most explanations either oversimplify it into a spreadsheet or bury it under so many caveats that people give up and blame their metabolism. This guide sits in the middle: what energy balance actually is, why the numbers are looser than they look, and how to set a deficit you can hold for months.

## Energy balance in one paragraph

Your body takes in energy from food and spends energy on staying alive, digesting, moving, and exercising. The total spent is your total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE. When intake matches TDEE, your weight is stable. When intake is lower, your body makes up the gap by drawing on stored energy, mostly body fat, some glycogen, and a little muscle. That gap is the deficit. No food, timing trick, or supplement lets you lose fat without one.

TDEE is made up of four parts:

- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)**: the energy your organs use at rest, usually 60 to 70 percent of the total.
- **Thermic effect of food**: the cost of digesting what you eat, roughly 10 percent, higher for protein.
- **Exercise activity**: deliberate training, which is a smaller slice than most people assume.
- **Non-exercise activity (NEAT)**: walking, fidgeting, standing, chores. This varies enormously between people and shrinks quietly when you diet.

You can estimate your TDEE with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/), but treat the result as a starting hypothesis, not a fact. Formulas are typically off by 10 to 15 percent, which is enough to matter.

## Why 7,700 kcal per kilogram is a rule of thumb, not a law

You have probably heard that a pound of fat contains 3,500 calories, or 7,700 per kilogram, so a 500-calorie daily deficit yields a pound a week. The figure comes from the energy density of adipose tissue and it is roughly right for the fat itself. The mistake is applying it as a fixed exchange rate over time.

It fails for three reasons:

1. **You do not lose pure fat.** Early weight loss includes water and glycogen, which are "cheap" in calorie terms. Later loss includes some lean tissue. The calories-per-kilogram figure shifts with the composition of what you lose.
2. **Your expenditure falls as you lose.** A 500-calorie deficit on day one is smaller by month three, because the body it is applied to has changed.
3. **The inputs are estimates.** Food labels are legally allowed to be off by up to 20 percent, portion sizes drift, and your TDEE was a formula in the first place.

Researchers who model weight loss dynamically rather than with the static rule consistently find that real-world loss over six to twelve months is roughly half of what the simple arithmetic predicts. That is not failure; it is the corrected arithmetic.

## Why weight loss slows down

Almost everyone loses faster in the first few weeks than afterward, and the slowdown is expected rather than a sign something broke.

**Metabolic adaptation.** When you eat less, your body spends a little less than its new size would predict. Hormones that regulate appetite and energy use, including leptin and thyroid hormones, drop modestly. The effect is real but smaller than the internet suggests: supervised studies find adaptive reductions of roughly 5 to 15 percent of expenditure, not a "starvation mode" that halts fat loss. In a genuine deficit you keep losing fat; the deficit is just smaller than you thought.

**Less mass to move.** A lighter body costs less energy to carry around, at rest and during activity. Losing ten kilograms can trim a couple of hundred calories from your daily expenditure through this alone.

**NEAT drops.** People in a deficit unconsciously move less: fewer stairs, more sitting, less fidgeting. This can be worth several hundred calories a day and is the most underrated reason progress stalls.

**Water and glycogen swings.** Each gram of stored glycogen holds about three grams of water. Cut carbohydrates and you drop a kilogram or two of water in days; eat a big carb meal and it returns. None of that is fat, in either direction.

## What a sensible deficit looks like

A larger deficit is not automatically better. Past a certain point you lose more muscle, train worse, sleep worse, and are far more likely to quit. The evidence-based sweet spot:

| Approach     | Deficit below TDEE | Expected loss per week   | Best for                                                                          |
| ------------ | ------------------ | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Conservative | 10–15%             | ~0.25–0.5% of bodyweight | People near their goal, athletes, anyone who has struggled with hunger            |
| Moderate     | 15–25%             | ~0.5–1% of bodyweight    | Most people, most of the time                                                     |
| Aggressive   | 25–35%             | ~1%+ of bodyweight       | Short phases for people with a lot to lose, ideally with high protein and lifting |

For an 80-kilogram person with a TDEE around 2,500 calories, a moderate deficit means eating roughly 1,900 to 2,100 calories and expecting 0.4 to 0.8 kilograms a week. People with more fat to lose can tolerate the higher end safely; leaner people should stay lower. As a rule, do not go below about 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men without clinical supervision. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or have a history of disordered eating, talk to a clinician before dieting at all.

## How to set your deficit

1. **Estimate your TDEE** with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/), being honest about activity. Most people overestimate it.
2. **Subtract 15 to 25 percent** to get your daily target.
3. **Set protein first**, around 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram, then fill the rest with carbohydrates and fat according to preference. The [macro calculator](/tools/macros/) does the arithmetic.
4. **Eat at that target for three to four weeks**, tracking as accurately as you reasonably can.
5. **Compare your average weekly weight change to the expected range.** If you are losing at the intended rate, do nothing. If not, adjust by 100 to 200 calories and reassess in another three weeks.

Your actual rate of loss is a far better measure of your true expenditure than any formula, and the [weight-loss timeline](/tools/timeline/) can show you what a realistic trajectory looks like once you have a few weeks of data.

## Why the scale lies week to week

Body fat changes slowly and steadily. Body weight changes quickly and noisily. A single weigh-in is affected by:

- Sodium from the previous day, which can hold onto a kilogram or more of water.
- Carbohydrate intake, via glycogen and its attached water.
- Whether you have had a bowel movement.
- Sleep, stress, and the cortisol that comes with both.
- Menstrual cycle phase, which can swing weight by one to three kilograms.
- A hard training session, via temporary muscle inflammation and fluid retention.

None of these are fat. A "gain" of a kilogram overnight would require eating roughly 7,700 calories above maintenance, which almost never happens. Weigh daily under the same conditions, average the week, and compare weekly averages. Two flat weeks is a signal worth acting on. Two days is not.

## Key takeaways

- Fat loss requires a calorie deficit. Every diet that works creates one; nothing else is doing the work.
- The 3,500-calorie-per-pound figure describes fat tissue, not your weight loss. Expect real-world loss to be slower than the simple math.
- Loss slows because you get lighter, move less, and adapt slightly. That is normal, not "starvation mode."
- Aim for 10 to 25 percent below TDEE and 0.5 to 1 percent of bodyweight per week, then calibrate from your real results.
- Judge progress by weekly averages over several weeks, never by a single weigh-in.
