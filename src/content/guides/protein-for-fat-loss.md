If you change only one thing about your diet when you start cutting calories, make it protein. It is the single macronutrient with the strongest evidence for improving the quality of weight loss: more of what you lose comes from fat, less from muscle, and you feel fuller doing it. The catch is that most people eat far less than the amount that delivers those benefits, and most advice about "how much" is either a vague percentage or a bodybuilding number pulled from the air. Here is what the research supports and how to hit it.

## The evidence-based range

For people who are in a calorie deficit and want to keep their muscle, the consensus from meta-analyses and position stands across sports nutrition bodies lands on **1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day**.

A few details matter:

- **1.6 g/kg is the floor**, not the target. A widely cited 2018 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that muscle gains from resistance training plateaued around 1.6 g/kg in people eating at maintenance. In a deficit, where the body has more reason to break down muscle, you want a margin above that.
- **2.2 g/kg is a comfortable ceiling** for most people. Going higher is not harmful for healthy kidneys, but the returns flatten out.
- **Lean people in aggressive deficits may benefit from up to ~2.4 g/kg.** Research on physique athletes and lean trainees on steep cuts suggests the higher end offers extra protection when there is little fat left to draw on and the deficit is large.
- **If you carry a lot of body fat, use goal weight or lean mass instead of total weight.** Scaling protein to 130 kilograms of total weight gives an impractical, unnecessary number. Use 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg of your target weight, or estimate lean mass with the [body-fat calculator](/tools/body-fat/) and aim for around 2.0 to 2.6 g/kg of that.

The [protein calculator](/tools/protein/) applies these rules for you. For a 75-kilogram person the answer is roughly 120 to 165 grams a day, which is two to three times what many people eat by default.

Anyone with kidney disease, who is pregnant, or who has a history of disordered eating should agree targets with a clinician rather than following a general range.

## Why protein preserves muscle in a deficit

When calories are short, your body needs energy from somewhere. Fat is the preferred source, but muscle protein is also on the table, particularly if dietary protein is low and you are not lifting. Muscle is metabolically expensive, and in a deficit the body is happy to shed expense.

Adequate protein counters this in two ways. It keeps the pool of amino acids topped up, so muscle protein synthesis continues even when energy is scarce, and it reduces the need to break down existing muscle to supply amino acids for other jobs like making enzymes and immune cells. Combine high protein with resistance training and studies routinely show people losing almost entirely fat, sometimes while gaining a little muscle, even on substantial deficits.

Preserving muscle matters beyond appearance. Muscle disposes of most of your glucose, holds up your resting metabolic rate, and lets you keep training hard. Losing ten kilograms of which three are muscle is a much worse outcome than losing eight kilograms of pure fat.

## Satiety and the thermic effect

Protein is the most filling macronutrient per calorie. Higher-protein meals reliably reduce hunger for hours afterward and lead people to eat less at their next meal without trying. That is an enormous advantage when calories are limited: 500 calories built around chicken and vegetables keeps you full far longer than 500 calories of pastry.

Protein also costs more to digest. The thermic effect of food is around 20 to 30 percent for protein, compared to 5 to 10 percent for carbohydrate and 0 to 3 percent for fat, so 100 calories of protein leaves you roughly 75 usable calories. Across a day the difference is modest, perhaps 50 to 100 calories, but it is free and stacks with the appetite effect.

## Spreading protein across the day

Total daily intake matters most, but distribution helps. Muscle protein synthesis appears to be maximally stimulated by roughly **0.4 grams per kilogram per meal**, after which additional protein in that sitting adds little. Spreading intake across **three to five meals** lets you hit that threshold repeatedly.

For an 80-kilogram person aiming for 160 grams a day, that looks like four meals of about 40 grams each, or three of 45 to 50 grams plus a snack. Two big meals is not a disaster, and older adults may need slightly more per sitting, but four evenly protein-rich meals is the easiest pattern to sustain.

## Common mistakes

**Counting protein "sources" that are mostly something else.** Nuts, peanut butter, hummus, and quinoa contain protein, but they deliver it wrapped in far more fat or carbohydrate. They are fine foods, just not efficient ways to hit your target in a deficit.

**Back-loading the whole day into dinner.** Skipping protein at breakfast and lunch, then eating a 100-gram steak, wastes the per-meal stimulus and leaves you hungry all afternoon.

**Chasing the number with powder alone.** Shakes are convenient, but whole foods bring fiber, volume, and micronutrients that keep you full. Use powder to fill gaps, not to replace meals.

**Ignoring total calories.** Protein does not cancel a surplus. If you eat 200 grams of protein and 3,500 calories, you will gain weight. Set your calorie target with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/) first, then build protein inside it with the [macro calculator](/tools/macros/).

**Assuming more is always better.** Above roughly 2.4 g/kg, extra protein displaces carbohydrates and fat that support training and hormones without adding benefit.

## Key takeaways

- Aim for 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg of bodyweight per day in a deficit; lean people on aggressive cuts can go to about 2.4 g/kg.
- If you carry a lot of fat, base the number on goal weight or lean mass instead of total weight.
- Protein preserves muscle, blunts hunger, and costs more calories to digest than any other macronutrient.
- Spread intake across three to five meals of roughly 0.4 g/kg each.
- Build every meal around a real protein source; nuts, grains, and spreads do not count for much.
- Protein works inside a calorie deficit, not instead of one.
