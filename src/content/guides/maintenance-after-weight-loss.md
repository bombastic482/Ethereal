Losing weight is the part everyone plans for. Keeping it off decides whether the loss meant anything, and it is where most people are left without a map. The diet ends, the rules dissolve, and within a year or two the weight is back, often with extra. That outcome is common but not inevitable, and the people who avoid it are not genetically special or unusually disciplined. They do a handful of specific, observable things. This guide is about those things, and about the first months after a diet when the habits that keep weight off either take root or do not.

## What the regain numbers actually say

Long-term follow-up studies of people who lose weight through diet and lifestyle change are consistent: on average, a third to half of lost weight is regained within a year, and most of it within three to five years. A substantial minority, around one in five, keep off most of what they lost long term.

Two things are worth noticing. First, "most of it" is an average, and averages hide the people at both ends. Second, one in five is not a small number. Registries following thousands of successful maintainers, people who have kept off at least 14 kilograms for more than a year, exist because these people are common enough to study. What they do is the most useful information in this subject.

## Why the body pushes back

After weight loss your body is not neutral about the change. Appetite hormones shift toward hunger for months, sometimes years. Expenditure is lower, because you are smaller and because of a modest adaptive component. Food is more rewarding. None of this is a broken metabolism; it is a system that evolved to defend against famine, doing its job.

So maintenance is not "going back to normal." Your old normal produced your old weight. Maintenance is a new normal, deliberately built, that balances intake and expenditure at your new size.

## Reverse dieting: the myth and the reality

Reverse dieting means increasing calories slowly after a diet, perhaps 50 to 100 a week, with the promise that this "rebuilds your metabolism" and lets you eventually eat far more without gaining. The myth is the metabolism part. There is no good evidence that a slow increase raises expenditure beyond what your new size and activity dictate. Adaptation reverses with time at a stable weight, not with the gradualness of the increase.

The reality is that a slow increase is still a sensible way to transition, for different reasons:

- It avoids the multi-kilogram jump from glycogen and water that comes from adding 800 calories overnight, which rattles people into panic-restriction.
- It gives you time to observe your real maintenance level rather than guessing.
- It keeps tracking in place during the transition, which is when most people quit tracking and lose the thread.

Think of it as finding maintenance carefully, not as a metabolic repair protocol.

## Finding your new maintenance

Your maintenance intake after a diet is lower than before, and probably a little lower than a formula predicts for your new weight. Start with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/) at your current weight and activity, subtract about 5 to 10 percent for adaptation, and treat that as a hypothesis.

Then test it:

1. Eat at that level for three to four weeks, tracking honestly.
2. Weigh daily and compare weekly averages in your [weight tracker](/track/). Expect a one- to two-kilogram rise in the first week from glycogen and water, which is not fat.
3. If the weekly average is stable after week two, you have found maintenance. If it keeps rising, drop 100 to 150 calories. If it keeps falling, add the same.

Over the following months, as hormones settle and activity recovers, your true maintenance will drift upward slightly. Recheck every few months. The [weight-loss timeline](/tools/timeline/) helps you tell the expected rebound from a real trend.

## What the successful minority do

Across registries and long-term trials, maintainers share a short, unglamorous list of behaviors.

- **They keep monitoring themselves.** Most weigh at least weekly, many daily. They know their numbers, and they notice drift early. The ones who stop weighing are the ones who regain.
- **They stay active.** Around an hour a day of mostly moderate activity, often walking, is typical. Activity is the strongest single predictor of maintaining a loss, more than any dietary pattern.
- **They eat a consistent, protein-forward diet.** Breakfast most days, similar meals on weekends and weekdays, and higher protein than the average person. The [protein calculator](/tools/protein/) target you used while dieting still applies.
- **They keep some structure around food.** Not a diet, but a set of defaults: what is in the kitchen, what a normal plate looks like, how often eating out happens.
- **They act fast on small regains.** They do not wait until five kilograms are back. They respond to one or two.
- **They watch screen time and sleep.** Less television, more sleep, which both correlate with maintenance independently of eating.

None of these require dieting forever. They require keeping the measurement and the movement, and letting food settle into a pattern you can keep for years.

## Set a regain threshold in advance

The single most protective decision you can make is to decide, before you need to, at what point you will act. A good rule is a threshold of 2 to 3 percent of your bodyweight above your maintenance weight, measured as a weekly average, not a single reading. For an 80-kilogram person that is around 82 kilograms.

When you cross it, you do not launch a new diet. You return to the structure that worked for two to four weeks: tracking, a modest 10 to 15 percent deficit, steps back up, sweetened drinks down. Then you return to maintenance. Write the threshold and the response into [your plan](/plan/) so the decision is made calmly now rather than emotionally later.

The [BMI calculator](/tools/bmi/) can give you a general reference range, but anchor your threshold to the weight you actually maintained, not to a chart.

## Identity: becoming someone who weighs this

Dieters who regain often describe the diet as something they did. Maintainers describe themselves differently: "I'm someone who walks every day," "I weigh in on Mondays," "I don't really keep candy in the house." The behavior has become part of who they are rather than a project they completed.

This changes decisions. A person on a diet, faced with cake, is resisting. A person who does not usually eat cake at work is just being themselves, and spends no willpower. You build this slowly, by noticing what you now do without thinking and naming it as yours.

If your weight history includes cycles of extreme restriction and regain, or if you have ever had an eating disorder, please involve a clinician or registered dietitian in planning maintenance; the strategies here are for people whose relationship with food is basically healthy.

## Key takeaways

- Most people regain much of their lost weight within a few years, but roughly one in five keep it off, and they do specific, learnable things.
- Reverse dieting does not "repair" your metabolism, but increasing calories gradually is still a sensible way to find your new maintenance level.
- Your new maintenance is lower than before. Estimate it, test it for three to four weeks, and adjust by 100 to 150 calories at a time.
- Maintainers keep weighing, stay active for around an hour a day, eat protein-forward and consistent meals, and respond to small regains quickly.
- Decide your regain threshold (2 to 3 percent of bodyweight) and your response now, before you need them.
- Maintenance works when the habits become who you are rather than something you did.
