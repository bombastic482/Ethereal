The scale was moving. You were doing everything the same. Then it stopped, and it has been stuck for a week, or two, or a month. Plateaus are the point where most diets quietly end, not because the person gave up but because they concluded the plan no longer worked and drifted. The frustrating truth is that most plateaus are not what they appear to be, and the ones that are real have a small number of well-understood causes. Diagnose first, then act.

## First, is it actually a plateau?

A plateau is not a week without change. Body weight fluctuates by one to two kilograms day to day from water, glycogen, sodium, and digestion, and a genuine fat loss of half a kilogram can hide inside that noise for a fortnight. Before you change anything, apply the **two-to-three-week rule**: compare your weekly average weight (not any single reading) over at least two, ideally three, consecutive weeks. If the average has not moved across that window, you have a stall worth investigating. If it has moved slowly, you have a slow loss, which is a different and much smaller problem.

The [weight-loss timeline](/tools/timeline/) is useful here because it shows what your expected trajectory looks like; a couple of flat weeks inside an otherwise falling trend is well within normal.

## The four usual suspects

### 1. Water retention

Fat can be leaving while water is arriving, and the scale only sees the sum. Common triggers:

- A new or harder training program, which causes muscle inflammation and fluid retention that can last one to two weeks.
- Higher sodium intake, restaurant meals, or a change in carbohydrate intake.
- Poor sleep and elevated stress, which raise cortisol and promote fluid retention.
- The luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, which can add one to three kilograms that vanish within days of a period starting.

The classic pattern is a "whoosh": weeks of no movement followed by a sudden drop of a kilogram or more overnight, as retained water is finally released. If your waist measurement or clothes are improving while the scale is flat, water is almost certainly the explanation.

### 2. Tracking drift

This is the most common cause of a real stall. Intake creeps upward while the numbers you write down stay the same. Untracked bites while cooking, oil that went from a teaspoon to a glug, "a handful" of nuts that grew, weekend meals estimated generously, a new brand of bread with a different label. None of it feels like cheating, and it can easily add 200 to 400 calories a day, enough to erase a moderate deficit.

The test is simple and slightly tedious: weigh everything you eat, including sauces, oils, and drinks, for one full week, including the weekend. Most people who do this find their stall explains itself.

### 3. NEAT has dropped

Non-exercise activity thermogenesis, the calories you burn through walking, standing, fidgeting, and everyday movement, falls in a deficit without you noticing. You take the lift, sit more, walk slower, skip the evening stroll. Studies that measure it find some people reduce daily expenditure by several hundred calories this way. Your training sessions are unchanged, your food is unchanged, and yet your deficit has shrunk.

Check your average daily step count against what it was when you started. If it has dropped by a few thousand, you have found a large part of the problem.

### 4. You are genuinely lighter and slightly adapted

This is the cause everyone jumps to first, and it is real, just smaller than feared. A lighter body needs fewer calories, and the body in a deficit becomes a few percent more efficient. Between the two, a deficit that was 500 calories at the start might be 250 calories after ten kilograms of loss. Fat loss halves, and the scale looks stuck relative to your earlier pace. Nothing is broken; the target simply needs updating.

## Lower calories or move more?

Once you have ruled out water and tracking drift, you need to reopen the gap. You have two levers.

**Move more first if** your step count has fallen, you sit most of the day, or your calories are already fairly low. Adding 2,000 to 3,000 daily steps restores expenditure without making meals smaller, and it tends to improve appetite regulation and sleep. It is also the easier lever to sustain.

**Lower calories if** your activity is already high and consistent, or you simply cannot add more movement to your day. Cut by 100 to 200 calories, not 500. Small reductions preserve training quality, and you can always cut again in three weeks. Recalculate with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/) using your new weight to sanity-check the number, and keep protein steady with the [protein calculator](/tools/protein/) so that the reduction comes from carbohydrate or fat rather than the macronutrient protecting your muscle.

Whichever you choose, change one thing and hold it for another two to three weeks before judging.

## When to take a diet break instead

Sometimes the right response to a stall is to stop dieting for a while. A diet break means raising calories to your estimated maintenance for one to two weeks, keeping protein and training the same. It is not a cheat week; it is planned, tracked eating at a higher level.

Consider one if:

- You have been in a deficit for more than 10 to 12 weeks without a break.
- Hunger, irritability, poor sleep, or flat training have become constant rather than occasional.
- You have already lost a large fraction of your goal and your rate of loss has slowed considerably.
- You feel your adherence slipping and "just one more week" is becoming a fantasy.

Research comparing continuous dieting with periodic maintenance breaks has found that people who take breaks lose at least as much fat over the full period, and some trials suggest they retain slightly more lean mass and expenditure. The bigger benefit is psychological: a fortnight of normal eating makes the next block of dieting feel possible. Expect the scale to rise a kilogram or two during the break from glycogen and water, and expect it to fall again in the first week back.

If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or have a history of disordered eating, changes to calories and diet duration are best made with a clinician rather than on your own.

## A simple decision sequence

1. Confirm the stall with at least two to three weeks of flat weekly averages.
2. Check for water: new training, sodium, sleep, cycle phase, waist trend.
3. Weigh everything for one honest week and compare against your target.
4. Check your step count against your starting baseline.
5. If all of that is clean, either add 2,000 to 3,000 steps or cut 100 to 200 calories. Not both at once.
6. If you have been dieting for months and feel worn down, take a one-to-two-week break at maintenance first.
7. Hold the change for another two to three weeks before reassessing.

## Key takeaways

- A plateau is two to three weeks of flat weekly averages, not a few stubborn days.
- Water retention from training, sodium, sleep, or menstrual cycle hides fat loss and often ends in a sudden "whoosh."
- Tracking drift and reduced everyday movement explain most genuine stalls; metabolic adaptation is real but modest.
- Change one variable at a time: add steps or trim 100 to 200 calories, then wait.
- After long stretches of dieting, a one-to-two-week break at maintenance is often the smarter move.
