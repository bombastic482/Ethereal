GLP-1 receptor agonists have changed what is possible for people who spent decades fighting their appetite. They work by making you less hungry, slowing digestion, and quieting the background noise about food, producing a large, relatively effortless calorie deficit. That is the point of the drug, and also the problem this guide is about. When appetite drops that far, protein intake usually falls with it, and the weight that comes off includes more muscle than it should. That is not a reason to avoid the medication; it is a reason to be deliberate about what you do eat. Nothing here replaces the clinician who prescribed yours; it is meant to help you have better conversations with them.

## What the medication changes

GLP-1 medications mimic a gut hormone that signals fullness. People describe three effects: hunger arrives later and weaker, normal portions feel like too much, and "food noise," the intrusive thinking about what to eat next, goes quiet. Many people eat 500 to 1,000 fewer calories a day without noticing and lose 10 to 20 percent of their bodyweight over a year or more.

The catch is that appetite suppression is not selective. You eat less of everything, including protein, fiber, and fluid. Early nausea also pushes people toward bland, low-protein foods: crackers, toast, plain rice. An 1,100-calorie diet built around those is a recipe for losing muscle.

## The lean mass findings

Any large weight loss includes some lean tissue; about a quarter of the weight lost is typical with diet alone. Body composition studies of people on GLP-1 medications find similar or somewhat higher proportions, often 25 to 40 percent of total loss. Part of that is water and organ tissue that scales with body size, but a meaningful share is skeletal muscle.

Muscle is what lets you climb stairs, carry groceries, and stay independent into older age, and it makes maintenance easier because it costs energy to keep. Lose a lot of weight with poor muscle retention and you end up lighter but with a higher body-fat percentage than the same loss done well. The [body fat calculator](/tools/body-fat/) is a blunt instrument but shows the direction over time.

The two things that reliably limit muscle loss during any large weight loss are adequate protein and resistance training. On a GLP-1 medication they are harder to achieve and more important.

## Protein: set a target and treat it as the priority

Aim for 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of goal bodyweight, roughly 90 to 130 grams a day for most people, toward the top if you lift. Use the [protein calculator](/tools/protein/) for a number, then make protein the first thing on the plate, because by the time you have eaten it you may not want anything else.

Practical patterns that work:

- **Protein first, every time.** Eat the chicken, eggs, fish, tofu, or yogurt before the vegetables and starch. If you stop early, you stopped after the important part.
- **Smaller, more frequent protein doses.** Three meals of 40 grams may be impossible; four or five of 20 to 30 grams are often fine.
- **Liquid protein when solid food is unappealing.** A shake, milk, kefir, or drinkable yogurt is far easier to get down on a queasy morning than a steak.
- **Keep soft, high-protein options stocked.** Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, eggs, soft tofu, canned fish, lentil soup, and protein puddings all go down easily.
- **Do not fill up on crackers.** Comfort carbohydrates are the default when nausea hits, and they crowd out everything else.

## Fiber, fluid, and the digestion problem

Slowed stomach emptying is part of how the medication works, and constipation is common. Eating very little makes it worse. Two targets help:

- **Fiber: 20 to 30 grams a day**, from vegetables, berries, oats, legumes, chia or ground flaxseed. Increase gradually; a sudden jump will bloat you.
- **Fluid: at least 2 liters a day**, more if you are active or it is hot. Reduced appetite also reduces thirst, and mild dehydration makes nausea, headaches, and constipation worse. The [water intake calculator](/tools/water/) gives you a personal figure.

If constipation persists, tell your prescriber; it is treatable.

## Eating through nausea

Nausea is most common in the first weeks and after each dose increase. Foods and habits that most people tolerate:

- Cold or room-temperature food, which smells less than hot food.
- Small portions eaten slowly; stop at the first sign of fullness rather than finishing the plate.
- Eggs, yogurt, cottage cheese, smoothies, soups with lentils or shredded chicken, oatmeal with protein powder stirred in.
- Ginger tea, plain crackers in small amounts alongside protein, not instead of it.
- Avoiding very fatty or fried food, which sits in a slowed stomach for hours.
- Not lying down right after eating.

If nausea stops you eating for more than a day or two, or you are vomiting, contact your clinician. Persistent inability to eat is a reason to adjust the dose, not to push through.

## Resistance training: the other half

Protein tells your body what to build with; lifting tells it what to keep. Two or three short full-body sessions a week, using bodyweight, bands, or dumbbells, is enough to reduce muscle loss during rapid weight loss. You do not need intensity or volume; you need consistency and progression. Start with squats to a chair, push-ups against a counter, rows with a band, and a carry or step-up, and add a little each week.

Energy may be low early on. Walking counts too and protects against the drop in incidental movement that comes with eating less, but it does not preserve muscle the way lifting does, so prioritize the strength sessions even if they are brief.

## Working with your prescriber

The medication is one part of a plan, and your prescriber or a dietitian can help with the rest. Things worth bringing up:

- Your protein intake and whether you are reaching your target.
- Any side effects that stop you eating adequately, particularly persistent nausea, vomiting, or constipation.
- Unusually rapid loss, such as more than 1.5 percent of bodyweight a week for several weeks, which may warrant a slower titration.
- Dizziness, fatigue, or weakness, which can signal under-eating or dehydration.
- Your long-term plan, including what happens if you stop the medication, when appetite typically returns.

Track your weight in the [weight tracker](/track/) and log a few typical days in [Today](/today/) so you arrive at appointments with real data. If you have a history of disordered eating, raise it; appetite suppression can complicate recovery.

## Key takeaways

- GLP-1 medications create a large deficit by reducing appetite, but they reduce appetite for protein, fiber, and fluid too.
- Studies find a quarter to 40 percent of weight lost can be lean mass. Protein and resistance training are what limit that.
- Aim for 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg of goal bodyweight in protein, eaten first at every meal, in smaller and more frequent doses if needed.
- Target 20 to 30 grams of fiber and at least 2 liters of fluid daily to manage constipation and nausea.
- Lift two or three times a week, even briefly. Walking helps but does not preserve muscle on its own.
- Keep your prescriber informed about protein intake, side effects, and pace of loss. This guide informs that conversation; it does not replace it.
