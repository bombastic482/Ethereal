Ask most people how to burn more calories and they will describe a workout: a run, a spin class, a circuit of burpees. Those sessions are real, but they account for a surprisingly small share of daily energy expenditure. The larger and more variable slice is everything else you do while upright: walking to the car, pacing on a call, cooking, carrying groceries, fidgeting. That category is non-exercise activity thermogenesis, and it explains more about why two people on the same diet lose at different rates than any workout plan does. This guide is about understanding it and protecting it.

## What NEAT is

Your total daily energy expenditure has four parts: basal metabolic rate, the thermic effect of food, planned exercise, and NEAT. The first two are largely fixed for a given body size. Exercise is bounded; even a hard hour burns a few hundred calories, and most people do not train daily. NEAT is everything else, and it is the only component that routinely swings by hundreds of calories a day without you noticing.

Between two people of the same size and gym habits, NEAT can differ by up to 2,000 calories a day in extreme cases, and by 400 to 800 in ordinary ones. A desk worker who drives and streams might accumulate 3,000 steps a day. A nurse, a server, or a parent of toddlers might hit 15,000 without ever "exercising." That gap dwarfs the difference between a 20-minute HIIT session and a 40-minute one.

| Activity profile  | Typical steps per day | Approximate NEAT above basal | What it looks like                              |
| ----------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Sedentary         | under 5,000           | 200–400 kcal                 | Desk job, drives, evenings on the couch         |
| Lightly active    | 5,000–7,500           | 400–600 kcal                 | Desk job plus a daily walk, some chores         |
| Moderately active | 7,500–10,000          | 600–900 kcal                 | Commutes on foot, active hobbies, standing work |
| Very active       | over 10,000           | 900–1,500+ kcal              | Physical job, young children, little sitting    |

The numbers are rough and depend on bodyweight and pace, but the shape is what matters. Moving from sedentary to moderately active is worth roughly the same as a daily workout, every day, with no recovery cost.

## Why NEAT collapses when you diet

Here is the part most diets never mention. When you eat less, your body quietly moves less. It is barely conscious: you take the elevator, sit while you wait, fidget less, choose the closer parking spot. Studies that measure movement with accelerometers during a deficit find NEAT drops by several hundred calories a day in some people, and the drop is larger on more aggressive deficits.

This is a major reason weight loss slows and plateaus happen. You calculate a 500-calorie deficit with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/), hold your intake perfectly, and lose nothing for three weeks. Your intake did not change; your expenditure did, from the one direction you were not watching. The fix is not to eat less again. It is to notice the drop and reverse it.

A step count is the simplest way to notice. It is not a perfect measure of NEAT, but it is cheap, objective, and catches the slide early. If you averaged 8,000 steps before dieting and 5,500 now, you have found your plateau.

## Step targets that make sense

The 10,000-step figure began as a marketing slogan, but research has been kind to it. Large observational studies show health benefits rising steeply from low counts up to around 7,000 to 8,000 steps a day, with gentler gains past 10,000. For fat loss, the useful range is similar.

- **Under 5,000**: sedentary. Any increase pays off disproportionately.
- **7,000 to 10,000**: the practical target for most people who are dieting. Enough to keep NEAT from sliding and to add a meaningful margin to your expenditure.
- **Over 12,000**: fine if your life produces it naturally, but chasing it on top of a desk job and a training program can eat into recovery and time.

Set a target relative to your current baseline, not to an abstract number. If you average 4,000, aiming for 7,000 is a real change. Aiming for 12,000 is a setup for quitting in two weeks. Record your weekly average alongside your weight in your [weight tracker](/track/) so you can see whether a stall in weight lines up with a dip in movement.

## Practical ways to raise it

The best NEAT is the kind you do not have to schedule. Build it into things you already do.

- **Walk during calls.** A 30-minute phone call is 2,500 to 3,000 steps if you are moving.
- **Walk after meals.** Ten to fifteen minutes after lunch and dinner adds 2,000 steps, helps blood sugar, and gives the meal a natural ending.
- **Park further away, or get off a stop early.** Small, repeated, automatic.
- **Stand or pace when you would otherwise sit and wait.** Kettles, microwaves, lines, loading screens.
- **Take the stairs by default.** Make the elevator the exception you have to justify.
- **Walk to do errands within a mile.** It is often faster than parking anyway.
- **Have a "floor."** Decide a minimum, such as 6,000 steps, below which you will not go to bed. On a low day, a 20-minute evening walk closes the gap.

Standing desks help a little, mostly by making it easier to move; standing still burns only marginally more than sitting. Walking is the lever you can actually pull.

## Where cardio actually fits

None of this means cardio is useless. It means cardio's role is narrower than it is sold as.

Formal cardio burns calories while you are doing it, and that is roughly all it does for fat loss. A 30-minute jog is 250 to 400 calories depending on size and pace. That is real, but it is also one large coffee drink, and it carries a cost: fatigue, appetite, time, and for some people a tendency to move less for the rest of the day. HIIT is time-efficient but hard to recover from alongside lifting, and the "afterburn" is tens of calories, not hundreds.

Cardio earns its place for other reasons:

- Cardiovascular health, which is independent of weight and matters more.
- Fitness and the ability to do things you enjoy.
- Mood, sleep, and stress regulation.
- A modest, reliable contribution to the deficit when you want one.

A sensible weekly structure for most dieters is two to four strength sessions to protect muscle, 7,000 to 10,000 daily steps to protect NEAT, and one to three moderate cardio sessions if you like them. The [weight-loss timeline](/tools/timeline/) will show that cardio's contribution is a few weeks over a diet, not a transformation. If you dislike it, skip it and walk more. Tracking the [body fat calculator](/tools/body-fat/) alongside weight will reassure you that the walking-heavy approach is losing fat, not muscle.

If you have a heart condition, joint problems, are pregnant, or have been inactive for a long time, check with a clinician before changing your activity levels substantially.

## Key takeaways

- NEAT, the energy you spend on everyday movement, varies by hundreds of calories a day between people and is a bigger lever than formal exercise for most dieters.
- NEAT drops quietly during a diet and is a leading cause of unexpected plateaus. Watch your steps, not just your intake.
- A step target of 7,000 to 10,000 is the practical range for fat loss. Set it relative to your current baseline.
- Build walking into things you already do: calls, meals, errands, waiting. Set a daily floor.
- Cardio is good for your heart, mood, and fitness, and it contributes to the deficit, but its fat-loss effect is modest and optional. Walking and lifting do the heavy lifting.
