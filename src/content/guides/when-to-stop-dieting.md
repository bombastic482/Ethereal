Nobody tells you when to stop. Diet culture treats fat loss as a direction rather than a phase, so people keep going long past the point where the deficit does more harm than good: training falls apart, sleep breaks, and the weight stalls anyway. Knowing when to stop is a skill most people never learn, which is why so many lose the same 10 kilograms three times. This guide covers the signs, the evidence on diet breaks and maintenance phases, how to transition using your weight trend, and the red flags that mean you should talk to someone.

## Signs the deficit has gone too far

A calorie deficit is a stress. A moderate one is tolerable and produces fat loss; past a certain depth or duration, the costs climb faster than the benefits. The signals are consistent:

- **Constant, intrusive hunger** that does not respond to protein, fiber, or volume. Thinking about food all day is not normal.
- **Training has deteriorated.** Lifts going backward for weeks, no appetite for sessions you used to enjoy.
- **Sleep is broken.** Waking at 3 a.m. hungry or wired is a classic sign of a deficit that has gone too long or too deep.
- **Mood is flat or irritable** and concentration is poor.
- **You feel cold all the time**, libido has vanished, or, for women, periods have become irregular or stopped. These signal hormonal downregulation.
- **Weight has stalled for four or more weeks** despite genuine adherence, and you are close to your goal.

One of these on a bad week means nothing. Three or four persisting for two weeks means the plan must change, and "trying harder" is the wrong change.

## Diet breaks and refeeds: what the evidence shows

A refeed is one or two days at or above maintenance, usually with extra carbohydrate. A diet break is one to two weeks at maintenance. The findings on both are modest but useful.

**Refeeds** restore glycogen, improve training for a few days, and provide psychological relief. Their hormonal effects, such as a brief bump in leptin, are small. They do not "reset your metabolism," but they can make the next week more tolerable.

**Diet breaks** have a slightly stronger case. Trials comparing continuous dieting with two weeks on, two weeks off at maintenance found similar or slightly better fat loss for the same total time in deficit, with less reduction in resting metabolic rate. Other trials found none. The honest summary: breaks do not speed fat loss, do not slow it, and make long diets much easier to finish. If you are dieting longer than about 12 weeks, a one- to two-week break every 8 to 12 weeks is a reasonable default.

A break is a planned return to maintenance calories with the same food quality, protein, and training, not a free-for-all. Set the number with the [calorie calculator](/tools/calories/), eat it, and keep weighing.

## Maintenance phases and the set point

Your body appears to defend a weight range. After a loss, appetite rises, spontaneous movement drops, and expenditure falls slightly more than the lost mass predicts. This is the "set point," though it behaves more like a settling range. Time spent stable at a new weight appears to help it move; continuous dieting keeps the body in a defended state.

This is the strongest argument for maintenance phases. Instead of dieting from 95 kilograms to 75 in one push, diet to 85, hold for two to three months, then diet again. During the hold, hunger normalizes, training improves, and you practice the habits that keep weight off. People who structure fat loss this way lose more slowly on paper and keep more off in practice.

The [weight-loss timeline](/tools/timeline/) will show that a 20-week goal takes perhaps 28 to 32 weeks with maintenance phases built in. That is not lost time; it is what makes the result stick.

## How to transition using the trend, not the calendar

The most reliable signal is your [weight tracker](/track/) trend, read over weeks rather than days.

1. **Confirm the stall is real.** Four weeks of flat weekly averages with honest tracking, not two noisy days.
2. **Decide whether this is a natural stopping point.** Within a few kilograms of your goal, dieting more than 12 weeks, or several of the signs above present? If yes to any, stop.
3. **Raise calories to estimated maintenance in one step**, usually 300 to 500 above your deficit intake. Expect a 1- to 2-kilogram jump in the first week from glycogen and water, not fat.
4. **Hold for at least two weeks and watch the trend.** Stable after the water jump means you have found maintenance. Rising, trim 100 to 150; falling, add 100 to 150.
5. **Stay there for 8 to 12 weeks** between phases, or indefinitely at your goal. Keep protein high, lift, and loosen tracking gradually.

The [body fat calculator](/tools/body-fat/) can help you decide whether another phase is warranted or whether you are done and the remaining work is building muscle at maintenance.

## Mental health red flags

Fat loss and disordered eating can look identical from the outside. The difference is the relationship, not the numbers. Take these seriously:

- Food rules that have become rigid and anxiety-producing rather than helpful.
- Avoiding social meals, or compensating afterward for eating in front of others.
- Exercising to earn or punish eating.
- Continuing to diet past a healthy weight because stopping feels like losing control.
- Binge episodes with shame, followed by renewed restriction.

If several are familiar, the problem is not your calorie target. Stop dieting, eat at maintenance, and talk to someone. Your clinician can refer you to a dietitian or psychologist who specializes in eating behavior; this is common and treatable.

## When to see a professional

Some situations need more than a guide:

- Periods stopped or irregular for more than two to three months.
- Persistent dizziness, fainting, heart palpitations, or hair loss.
- Weight loss that continues well below a healthy range, or that you cannot stop.
- Diabetes, thyroid disease, or other conditions where medication may need adjusting as weight falls.

Arrive with your weight trend, a few weeks of food logs, and the signs you have noticed.

## What to do instead

Stopping a diet is a change of goal, not the end of the project. At maintenance, keep protein around 1.6 grams per kilogram, train to get stronger, sleep seven or more hours, and loosen tracking until you eat by habit rather than by spreadsheet. Set [your plan](/plan/) to a maintenance target and let it be boring for a while. Boring is what maintenance is supposed to feel like.

## Key takeaways

- Persistent hunger, failing training, broken sleep, flat mood, and lost periods mean the deficit has gone too far. Trying harder is the wrong response.
- Diet breaks do not speed fat loss but make long diets easier to finish. Plan one to two weeks at maintenance every 8 to 12 weeks.
- Maintenance phases let your body settle at a new weight and are the strongest predictor of keeping it off.
- Transition by raising calories to estimated maintenance in one step, expecting a small water jump, and adjusting by the weekly trend.
- Rigid rules, food fear, compensatory exercise, and binge-restrict cycles are mental health red flags. Stop and talk to a professional.
